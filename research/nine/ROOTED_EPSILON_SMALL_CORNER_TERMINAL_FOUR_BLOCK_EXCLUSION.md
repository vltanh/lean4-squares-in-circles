### 1.1. Vertex support

Write the actual inward orthonormal edges at p as u,v. The point p
is not a circle point because r<d<R.

Suppose an adjacent corner p+u were on the circle. Containment of
the opposite corner p+u+v and of p respectively gives

    p.v<=-1/2, p.u>=-1/2.

Writing x=p.u and y=p.v, the circle equation is
R^2=(x+1)^2+y^2. Hence (x+1)^2<=R^2-1/4=(d+1)^2 and
x+1>=1/2, so x<=d. Consequently

    r^2=R^2-2x-1>=d^2+1/4,

contrary to r<d. The reflected adjacent corner p+v is excluded by
the same proof. Thus the only possible physical circle corner is
the actual opposite corner Z=p+u+v.

Because the nonzero original G is supported on actual circle corners,
G is a positive multiple of this Z. Rewrite (4) as

    p+L n=lambda Z, lambda>0, |Z|=R.

There is no assumption that q belongs to E. Directly,

    p.Z/R-h
      =(R-n.Z)(lambda+L/R)>=0.

Also |Z-p|^2=2 gives p.Z=(R^2+r^2-2)/2. This proves (5).
The proof includes L=0 and either endpoint of the normal cone.

### 1.2. Side-interior support

Now E owns n, and in its actual (n,Jn) frame it is

    [h,h+1] times [z,z+1].

Each far-side corner has squared radius larger by 2h+1>0 than its
near-side partner. Neither near-side corner can therefore be a
physical circle corner. If both far corners were physical circle
corners, their transverse coordinates would be -1/2 and 1/2, and

    (h+1)^2+1/4=R^2,

so h=d. This contradicts r>=h and r<d.

Thus there is a unique actual circle corner Z on the far side.
Again (4) becomes p+L n=lambda Z with lambda>0. Its normal
coordinate gives

    lambda=(h+L)/(h+1)>=h/(h+1),

while the transverse coordinate gives p.Jn=lambda Z.Jn. Hence

    r^2=h^2+lambda^2(Z.Jn)^2
       >=h^2 R^2/(h+1)^2.

Since R>r, rearrangement gives (6). Side endpoints belong to the
vertex case above. In particular no physical two-circle mask is
dropped merely because one of its original weights might vanish;
the actual geometry of that mask was excluded first.
