We first prove the two simple drawing facts at the full original

## 1. The original selected graph is plane when drawn at its centers

For every two distinct actual unit centers p,q,

    |p-q|>=1,                                           (1)

because the two open incircles of radius one half are disjoint.
If their physical units touch, a common point and their actual
half-diagonals give

    |p-q|<=sqrt(2).                                     (2)

Draw every selected positive body edge as the straight segment
joining its actual centers. No such segment contains another
body center: (1) would give its length at least two, contradicting
(2). Distinct collinear segments cannot overlap except at a common
graph endpoint, by the same observation.

Suppose two nonincident drawn edges cross properly. Their four
endpoints form a convex quadrilateral whose diagonals are those
two edges. At each quadrilateral vertex the two adjacent sides
have lengths at least one by (1), while the opposite diagonal
has length at most sqrt(2) by (2). The cosine law therefore
makes each of its four interior angles at most pi/2. Their sum
is 2pi, so all four are exactly pi/2. Each diagonal then has
squared length at least 1+1 and at most two. All four sides
are consequently exactly one: the centers form an exact unit
square.

In this equality case the four physical units are the aligned
four-quadrant configuration at the center of that center square.
Here is the actual rigidity argument. Two disjoint units at
center distance one admit a separating unit normal n. Their
center difference has n projection at least the sum of their
support halfwidths, which is at least one. Equality in the
center-distance bound forces n to be their center-difference
direction and both halfwidths to equal one half. Thus that
direction is an own side axis of both units. Apply this to
the four adjacent center pairs. Their physical frames are the
same, and all four units meet at the common central junction.

The two crossing center segments are exactly its two opposite-
pair contacts. Sections 1--5 of
`PHYSICAL_BODY_GRAPH_UNCROSSING.md` ensure that the selected
positive graph retains at most one such opposite pair at any
four-square junction. Later thinning and aggregation cannot
restore the other one. This contradicts the two proposed
positive edges. Therefore no crossing occurs.

This proves that the straight center drawing of the unchanged
selected graph is a plane embedding. No new stress or normal
has been chosen; the already required physical uncrossing is
the only selection input. The argument does not claim this for
the full physical touching graph, which may retain both zero-
weight opposite contacts at a four-square junction.

## 2. Every physical boundary center is exposed in this drawing

The actual contained-unit center bound gives

    |c_T|<M,       M=91/60.

    |p|>=R-sqrt(2)/2>5/4.                              (3)

    |q-p|^2<=|q|^2-|p|^2
             <(91/60)^2-(5/4)^2=166/225<1,

contradicting (1). Thus p is a strict maximizer of the linear

exposed vertex of their convex hull and is incident to the
unbounded face of the straight center drawing. The same is
true after restricting that drawing to any subgraph retaining
p. This uses physical boundary status alone, even when its
total circle force or an individual corner weight vanishes.

Restrict now to the actual diamond on H,K,B,C. Both B,C are
exterior by (3). Its direct edge HK cannot be on the unbounded
face: in this biconnected plane diamond, an outer face containing
HK would be one of the triangles HKB or HKC, and would put
the other page strictly inside. Consequently the two bounded
triangular faces HKB and HKC lie on opposite sides of the
straight HK segment. In particular the two actual centers c_B
and c_C lie in opposite open half-planes of the line through
c_H,c_K. The triangles are nondegenerate, since three pairwise
distances in [1,sqrt(2)] cannot be collinear.

## 3. The actual center lens puts every boundary page on one side

Because H,K meet D_(1/60), their actual centers satisfy

    |c_H|,|c_K|<=1/60+sqrt(2)/2<29/40=:sigma.           (4)

The last strict comparison uses sqrt(2)/2<17/24. Rotate about
the unchanged origin and choose the axis signs and hub labels

    c_H=(x-a,w),       c_K=(x+a,w),
    a>=1/2,           x>=0,           w>=0.

The distance bound (1) supplies a>=1/2. The farther hub in (4)
gives

    x+a<sigma,
    0<=x<9/40,
    0<=w<sqrt(sigma^2-1/4)=21/40.                      (5)

Let p=(X,Y) be the center of any physical unit touching both
H and K, and put t=|X-x|. Both of its real contact distances
satisfy (2), so their maximum gives the exact lens inequality

    (a+t)^2+(Y-w)^2<=2.                               (6)

Suppose p lies on the origin side of the hub line, that is,
Y<=w. If Y<=0, then Y^2<=(Y-w)^2, and

    |p|^2
      <=x^2+t^2+2xt+(Y-w)^2
      <=2-a^2+x^2-2(a-x)t
      <=2-a^2+x^2
       <7/4+(9/40)^2=2881/1600.                       (7)

The penultimate step is valid because a-x>1/2-9/40>0.
If instead 0<=Y<=w, equations (5)--(6) give

    |X|<=x+t<=x+sqrt(2)-a
          <sigma+sqrt(2)-1<137/120,
    |Y|<=w<21/40.

Here sqrt(2)<17/12 was used in the final rational comparison.
Consequently

    |p|^2<(137/120)^2+(21/40)^2=11369/7200.             (8)

Both bounds are strictly smaller than (27/20)^2. The exact
positive reserves are respectively 7/320 and 1753/7200.

But every physical boundary center in the stated high/critical
radius range satisfies

    |p|>=R-sqrt(2)/2>103/50-71/100=27/20.              (9)

Apply this conclusion to the two actual pages B,C. They would
lie on the same side of the hub center line, contradicting
Section 2's opposite-side conclusion. This proves the theorem.
