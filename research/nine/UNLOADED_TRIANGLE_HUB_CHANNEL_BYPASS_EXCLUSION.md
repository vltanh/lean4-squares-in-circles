## 2. Strict support span for an actual unit

We first prove the geometric fact used twice below. Let W be an
actual unit square, let P be any minimum-m support point, and let
m,k be unit vectors with m.k<0. Then

    max_{x in W} k.(x-P) < 1.                              (2)

If P is a vertex, choose the two orthonormal inward axes u,v there,
so W=P+[0,1]u+[0,1]v and m.u,m.v>=0. Since m.k<0, at least one of
k.u,k.v is strictly negative. The positive k span is therefore at
most the other component when that component is positive, and is
zero otherwise. A positive component equal to 1 would make k the
corresponding inward axis, giving m.k>=0. Hence the positive span
is strictly smaller than 1. This argument includes a vertex that
is also an endpoint of a minimum-support side.

If P lies in a side's relative interior, the inward side normal is
m. Its k projection is strictly negative. The only possible positive
span comes from the tangent interval of length 1. Its unit tangent
has k projection of absolute value strictly below 1, because k has
a nonzero component along m. The projected interval from the actual
P is at most this absolute component. Again the span is strictly
below 1. Thus (2) retains every side mean and both side endpoints.

No physical circle or stress equation is used in (2).

## 3. A physical triangle at an owned root axis

Suppose actual units U,X,T are pairwise physically touching. Let
v be an actual U-outward separating unit normal toward one of X,T,
and let z be the actual U-outward normal toward the other. If v is
an own side axis of U, then

    v.z >= 0.                                             (3)

Indeed, suppose v.z<0 and put H=h_U(v). The width of U along its
own axis v is exactly 1. Every maximum-z support point of U then
lies on its minimum-v face. For the original U-to-other-neighbor
application P this gives v.P=H-1. The same P is a minimum-z support
point of that other unit. Apply (2), with k=v: every point of that
unit has v projection strictly below H. But every point of the
first neighbor lies in the opposite closed half-plane v.x>=H.
Those two neighbors are closed-disjoint, contrary to their actual
physical contact. This proves (3).

Equality in (3) is retained. The result requires one normal to be
an own axis of U; it makes no nonobtuseness assertion about two
arbitrary nonaxis normals. A shared side, an own-side endpoint and
an arbitrary original own-side mean all lie within the proved case.

Sections 2--3 reproduce the radius-free support-span and triangle
arguments, so no external triangle selection theorem is needed.
