## 1. Uniform cap theorem for every actual contained square

    -7/10<y<7/10 for every actual point (x,y) of K.      (1)

force, mask, contact point, mean or center-direction assumption.

Choose a highest actual corner Z. Its inward side axes can be written

    u=(-c,-s), v=(s,-c),
    c,s>=0, c^2+s^2=1,
    K=Z+[0,1]u+[0,1]v.

The axis-parallel endpoints are included by choosing the appropriate
highest corner. The actual rightmost and leftmost vertices are Z+v
and Z+u. Hence

    Z_x+s<=-1,
    c+s=width_x(K)<=R-1<13/12.                       (2)

The last comparison follows from R<25/12; its squared reserve is
625/144-1105/256=55/2304>0.

If c,s were both at least 1/10, concavity of
x+sqrt(1-x^2) on [1/10,sqrt(99)/10] would give

    c+s>=(1+sqrt(99))/10>13/12,

contrary to (2). The strict comparison uses sqrt(99)>59/6,
whose squared reserve is 83/36>0. Thus

    min(c,s)<1/10.                                   (3)

Suppose Z_y>=7/10. If s<1/10, the actual corner Z+u has

    (Z+u)_x<=-1-(c+s)<=-2,
    (Z+u)_y>3/5.

It would have squared norm strictly greater than

    4+9/25=109/25=1105/256+279/6400,

contradicting containment. If c<1/10, then s>99/100, and

    Z_x<=-1-s<-199/100<-79/40.

The actual highest corner itself would then have squared norm
strictly greater than

    (79/40)^2+(7/10)^2=281/64=1105/256+19/256,

again impossible. This proves max_K y<7/10. Reflecting the
actual coordinates vertically proves min_K y>-7/10, establishing
the whole cap theorem, including both orientation endpoints.
